# Colour and contrast

Colour and contrast are the first things a reader's eye reads. *Web Design in 4 Minutes* starts with black on white, then softens the body to a more comfortable shade (`#555`) and darkens the important words (`#333`). A primary accent colour is used for links, and secondary colours support borders, backgrounds, and subtle calls to action. The result is a palette that is useful, not merely decorative.

## Core lessons from the source

- Avoid pure black on white for body text. The harsh contrast can be tiring. A slightly softer body text with darker headings creates a more polished, comfortable reading experience.
- Use contrast for hierarchy. Headings and strong text should be darker than body text. This creates a clear order without relying on size alone.
- One primary accent. A single primary colour identifies interactive elements and the site's identity. It should be used consistently and with restraint.
- Secondary colours support, don't shout. Use subtle shades for borders, backgrounds, and code blocks. The accent should not be the loudest thing on every page.
- Colour carries meaning. Links, calls to action, and section identity should be reinforced by colour. If a colour does not have a job, it is noise.
- Accessibility is part of the palette. Contrast must be strong enough for readability. *Web Design in 4 Minutes* keeps contrast strong even as it softens the body text.

## $10k vs $500

| $500 | $10k |
|---|---|
| Pure black on pure white everywhere | Softer, chosen shades that are easy on the eyes while keeping contrast strong |
| Random accent colours | A primary accent and a few complementary secondary shades |
| Colour used without purpose | Colour reinforces meaning: links, calls to action, and section identity |
| Contrast too low for accessibility | Contrast supports both taste and accessibility |
