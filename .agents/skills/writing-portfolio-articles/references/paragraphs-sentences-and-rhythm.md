# Paragraphs, Sentences and Rhythm

## Paragraphs

Ask every paragraph why it is not part of the paragraph before or after it. A boundary may earn its place through a change of subject, time, scene, speaker, claim, evidence, scale, rhetorical function or pace.

One-sentence paragraphs are suspicious, not forbidden. Keep one when isolation creates emphasis, transition, revelation, relief or a deliberate change of pace. Repeated isolation becomes mannerism; forced combination becomes obstruction.

## Sentences

Ask every full stop why the thought is not better joined by a comma, semicolon, colon, conjunction or another adjunctive construction. This is a diagnostic question, not a target for maximum conjunction.

Keep the boundary when it serves meaning, pace, emphasis, clarity or voice:

> Sometimes, shoot the hostage. In Speed, Jack Traven's answer to a gunman using a hostage as leverage is to shoot the hostage. Problem changed.

The first sentence states the tactic. The second supplies the cultural mechanism. The fragment lands the consequence. Joining them would explain the relationship while killing the cadence.

Join a false boundary when one thought has been split into declarations:

> The code could show what we built; it couldn't reliably tell the next engineer why.

The semicolon makes the contrast one thought. Read both versions aloud and choose by effect, not punctuation count.

Vary sentence length because thought and breath vary. Split a long sentence when conjunction hides hierarchy; retain it when accumulation or qualification is the point.
