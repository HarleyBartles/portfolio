# Composition

## Focal points

A focal point is the place the eye lands first. A page or section should have one clear focal point. Multiple focal points compete and create noise.

| Technique | How to use it |
|---|---|
| Size | Make the most important element the largest. Scale should jump by at least one full step on the type or spacing scale. |
| Weight | Use a heavier weight for the primary heading or key figure. |
| Position | Place the focal point in the top-left or center of the visible area, depending on the script. |
| Isolation | Give the focal point more surrounding space than its neighbors. |
| Contrast | Use a color or texture sparingly to mark the focal point. Do not contrast more than one or two elements per section. |

## Visual hierarchy

Hierarchy tells the reader what to read next. Build it with three or fewer levels per section.

| Level | Role | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | The main message of the section | Size, weight, position, isolation |
| Secondary | Supporting points or calls to action | Size, weight, color, proximity |
| Tertiary | Captions, metadata, helper text | Subdued color, smaller size, lighter weight |

- Let the difference between levels be obvious. A reader should identify the primary, secondary, and tertiary levels in a glance.
- Do not use more than three heading treatments. More levels dilute the message.
- Keep the relationship stable across breakpoints. The hierarchy should hold at the smallest and largest sizes.

## Balance

Balance does not mean symmetry. It means the visual weight is distributed so the page feels stable.

- Large, dense elements feel heavier. Give them more room or place them lower in the composition.
- Empty space counts as weight. A large empty area balances a dense area if the proportion is intentional.
- Asymmetry creates motion. Use it to guide the eye from the focal point to the next important element.
- Alignment is the easiest way to create balance. Unaligned edges create tension that should be deliberate.

## Guiding the eye

The path from focal point to supporting content should be clear and short.

- Place the next most important element close to the focal point.
- Use the reading direction. In left-to-right layouts, the eye moves left to right and then down.
- Use implied lines. Alignment, shared margins, and repeated shapes create invisible paths.
- Avoid adding arrows, underlines, or motion as the primary guide. If the layout cannot lead the eye on its own, fix the layout first.
