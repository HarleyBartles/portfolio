# Interpret poll results

Load this reference when a run completes or when deciding whether another study would answer an unresolved editorial question.

## Read the result against the question

Describe what the observed choices show under the tested conditions, then distinguish that from editorial inference. Return to the article and read each flagged passage in context. A poll locates a question for human editorial judgment; it does not authorize a rewrite, rank quality or decide publication.

Report the denominator for every claim. Readers who saw an item, chose to enter, read closely, skimmed, deferred, received an end offer, opened later or answered a follow-up are different groups. Break down relevant results by condition and archetype, then inspect individual journeys when an aggregate hides different paths.

Keep satisfied stops distinct from lost-interest departures. A satisfied stop in the middle may mean that a beat resolves the reader's question too early; at the end it may mean the article delivered its promise. Decide by reading the beat and its transition, not by treating every stop as a failure.

## Respect selection and comparison limits

Opening or returning to an optional read is self-selection. Do not claim that reading it caused a change in satisfaction by comparing openers with non-openers. Report the denominator that received each offer and distinguish an unseen first offer from a deferred re-offer.

For paired drafts or conditions, use the same frozen cohort and compare matched completed journeys. State unmatched or incomplete counts. Keep stimulus and wording constant except for the intended variable. If wording, history, route or response order changed, treat that as a study-design change and do not attribute differences to a single other variable.

Interpret results only for the selected profiles and choices under those conditions. A wider cohort can reveal more variety in these model-generated responses, but cohort size alone does not establish precision, prevalence or representativeness. If a result suggests a different question, form a new hypothesis before designing another run.
