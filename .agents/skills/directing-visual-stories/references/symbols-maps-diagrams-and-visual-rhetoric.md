# Symbols, maps, diagrams, and visual rhetoric

Use this route when a composition explains relationships, represents space, encodes
evidence, or borrows the authority of maps, diagrams, interfaces, scientific
illustration, or symbolic systems. Representation is selective; the omissions and
encoding rules are part of the story.

## Define the represented truth

State what the visual claims to show and what it cannot show. Choose scale and
abstraction for the audience question: overview, route, comparison, topology,
quantity, causality, uncertainty, or procedure. More detail is not automatically
more truthful.

For maps, distinguish geographic distance from topological relation. Name
projection, orientation, scale, boundary, centre, landmark, route, and omitted
territory when they affect meaning. A game map may deliberately distort distance
for legibility, but the distortion must preserve the promised navigation.

For diagrams, decide whether position, connection, enclosure, direction, size,
colour, or sequence carries each relation. Do not encode two incompatible meanings
with one visual variable. Show uncertainty, missing evidence, or contested
boundaries rather than styling them as settled facts.

## Group and separate

Use figure-ground, proximity, similarity, continuation, enclosure, common region,
alignment, and closure to make relationships visible before labels are read. Break
a grouping rule only when the exception is the evidence. Test in monochrome,
reduced detail, and without decorative styling.

Symbols need a stable vocabulary, sufficient distinction, and a legend or context
when recognition is not assured. A symbol can operate as synecdoche, affiliation,
warning, state, or route marker, but cultural meaning is not universal. Verify
authority before assigning colour, gesture, clothing, emblem, or motif a social or
sacred meaning.

## Audit visual authority

Ask who owns the view and who becomes data, specimen, territory, target, or
spectacle. Scientific, documentary, cartographic, surveillance, and interface
languages can make a constructed selection look neutral. Record the evidence,
transformation, simplification, and point of view.

Accessible description is also a composition check. If the intended hierarchy and
relations cannot be described in a stable spatial or narrative order, the visual
may be relying on accidental placement. Creative direction should provide the
meaningful order and sensory intent; downstream accessibility skills own standards,
implementation, and testing.

### Route output

Record: claim and audience question; representational form; scale and abstraction;
encoded variables; grouping laws; symbol vocabulary; uncertainty/omissions;
viewpoint and power relation; accessible descriptive order; and the truth invariant
that must survive styling, responsive reflow, or interaction.

### Avoid

- treating a map or diagram as neutral because it looks technical;
- adding detail that exceeds the evidence or audience need;
- relying on colour alone or unverified cultural symbolism;
- making decorative connections imply unsupported causality;
- allowing responsive reflow to change the represented relationship.
