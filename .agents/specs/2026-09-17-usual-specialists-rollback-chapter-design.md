# The Usual Specialists V2: Rollback Chapter Design

**Status:** Approved chapter design; spec-readiness green; JIT plan waits for the actual Klause milestone output

**Parent page spec:** `2026-09-17-usual-specialists-visual-story-design.md`

**Chapter job:** Preserve recoverability when the chosen plan fails.

## Settled story contract

Klause has just committed the crew to one plan. Rollback's chapter immediately
treats that certainty as something that can fail.

Rollback provides Plans B, C, D through Z and the plan for when all the named plans
burn, but his deeper job is not alphabetic contingency. He is containment,
recovery, abort conditions and restore paths made into a person. He has seen more
failed plans and stranger disasters than anybody else in the safehouse and assumes
failure is a normal state that competent systems must survive.

The governing character sentence is:

**the environment breaks; Rollback doesn't.**

The chapter's visual proposition is stronger still:

**the audience begins inside the failure; Rollback closes it off; the audience
returns on the recoverable side.**

His key question remains:

`What's your Plan B?`

The chapter must make clear that this is the question Patch came to have asked, not
a gotcha proving Patch incompetent.

## Audience change

The reader should move from "we have a committed plan" to "failure no longer means
loss of the crew's ability to recover."

Klause makes a decision singular. Rollback makes commitment survivable.

Silk creates chaos deliberately to test a route. Rollback absorbs chaos, contains
it and preserves a safer state from which the crew can continue. That distinction
is essential.

## Primary visual family and viewing contract

**Primary family:** encounter/sequence.

**Supporting families:** internal-frame/occlusion and mass/stillness.

**Viewing contract:** directed pictorial sequence. Camera position is story logic,
not coverage. The opening places the audience on the failure side of reinforced
glass. The shutter removes the safe-side figures from view and takes the sequence
to black. The next visible beat returns on the opposite, recoverable side of the
containment boundary.

Do not turn the chapter into a branching B-through-Z infographic or a tour of
equally weighted backup rooms. Recovery depth is proved through containment logic,
Rollback's experience and his immediate movement toward the next failure test.

## Safehouse territory and spatial pressure

Rollback has taken the apartment basement because its rebar-concrete walls make it
the obvious place to run containment and recovery tests.

The environment should feel **constrained, contained and almost stifling** without
becoming cramped in a comic way. It is a heavy volume built to hold force. Ceilings,
walls, glazing, shutter tracks, conduits and control surfaces should make the room
feel structurally bounded.

At the same time, the basement comfortably accommodates Rollback. He is at the
outer boundary of believable autonomous-agent scale: very large, broad, sturdy and
load-bearing in his silhouette, but not a freak of nature and not human-scale. The
space has enough clearance for him because a containment environment designed by
Rollback would obviously account for its largest operator.

Use:

- reinforced/rebar concrete;
- thick observation glass;
- heavy steel shutter tracks and seams;
- practical containment controls;
- dense but orderly service conduits and recovery infrastructure;
- olive, charcoal, steel and warm amber material language; and
- enough apartment-basement continuity to keep this part of the same safehouse.

This is industrial recovery space, not a military bunker, weapons room, command
bridge, villain lair or generic sci-fi control room.

## Rollback's physical presence

Rollback is the largest believable agent in the room, broad and planted with a low
centre of gravity. His size expresses reliability rather than threat.

He moves. He observes. He operates controls. He walks on to the next test. But
nothing about him should read as hurried, startled or theatrically heroic. He has
seen this class of failure before, and probably several worse variants Patch would
not believe if told about them.

His calm is not passivity. It is experienced containment practice.

## Motion and stillness contract

The chapter gets continuous life from **subtle environmental state**, not from
busy character animation.

The preferred ambient motion is an amber warning light that slowly colour-cycles
or flashes through the field approximately once every **5–10 seconds**. That alone
is enough to buy motion in an otherwise solid, reliably static Rollback chapter.

The motion hierarchy is:

1. **amber warning state** — slow, sparse environmental pulse;
2. **shutter descent** — the single decisive mechanical movement;
3. **small facial changes** — Rollback's diagnosis and realisation;
4. **dog-tag snatch/drop** — the single sharp personal assent action; and
5. **Rollback walking on** — return to ordinary containment work.

Do not animate Rollback continuously to prove he is alive. His relative stillness
is the contrast that gives the environmental movement and the dog-tag action
weight.

Reduced motion must preserve the same story with a static warning-state treatment.
The warning pulse is atmosphere/state evidence, not a dependency for comprehension.

## Approved authored sequence

### 1. Failure-side observation

Begin **inside the active containment chamber**, looking outward through thick
reinforced observation glass.

Do not show the experiment or meltdown directly. It is behind the camera. Its state
is inferred from what reaches the glass and room: amber light cycling across the
surface, subtle reflection, haze, heat/state change or similarly restrained
evidence.

Through the glass, Rollback's huge stoic face and planted body are the dominant
safe-side read. Patch is behind or slightly offset from him, still pitching. The
failure has Rollback's operational attention without creating panic.

This opening is containment as point of view: the reader occupies the state that
needs to be bounded.

### 2. Seen enough

Rollback reaches his threshold for intervention.

No theatre. No alarmed reaction. No scramble. He has seen enough information to
classify the state, so he presses the containment control.

His physical action should be economical enough that the resulting environmental
change feels much larger than the gesture that caused it.

### 3. Shutter to black

The heavy steel shutter descends **between the audience and Rollback**.

Because the camera remains on the chamber side, Rollback and Patch disappear behind
the closing boundary. The safe-side observation slit narrows until the field is
fully occluded.

**Black.**

Hold the black as a real beat, not a decorative wipe.

Over black, carry the simple text:

`What's your Plan B?`

The chapter has demonstrated the purpose of the question before asking it.

### 4. Return on the recoverable side

Fade up **outside** the containment chamber. The audience is now on the same side
of the boundary as Rollback and Patch. The closed shutter is a heavy contained mass
behind them rather than something closing the audience in.

Patch answers simply:

`We don't have one.`

This is not embarrassed confession. Patch knows what capability he came here to
recruit.

### 5. Diagnosis

Close on Rollback. A slight sneer, sceptical curl or equivalent tiny expression is
enough.

`Then you ain't got a plan.`

The line is a veteran's factual diagnosis, not humiliation. In Rollback's world, a
plan without a recoverable failure state is structurally incomplete.

### 6. Realisation

Beat.

Patch gives the smallest wry smile or equivalent reaction. He does not defend the
missing Plan B.

Rollback clocks what that means: Patch has not accidentally failed the interview.
The absent recovery layer is exactly why Patch came to him. This is Rollback's
internal `you son of a gun` moment.

The realisation should live in expression/timing. Rollback does **not** need to say
the recurring assent phrase aloud.

### 7. Dog-tag assent

Rollback's one sharp personal movement follows immediately. He snatches the chain
from his own neck and drops the two scuffed metal dog tags into Patch's waiting
hands or open recruitment folder.

The imagined **clank** determines the visual timing even if the shipping page is
silent.

One tag carries `you son of a gun`. The other carries `I'm in`. The two tags are
therefore the durable material completion of the thought that began in Rollback's
realisation beat.

There is no sentimental presentation and no second confirmation state.

### 8. The next failure

Rollback moves on.

He heads toward the next containment chamber because another plan is waiting to be
destroyed, observed and made recoverable when it fails. This is the proof that his
depth goes beyond a finite list of alphabetic backups. The opening disaster was not
a special crisis staged for Patch. This is Rollback's ordinary work.

Patch is left with the assent material while Rollback has already resumed his
operating rhythm.

## Why recovery depth is not an infographic

Do not visualise Plans B through Z as a tree, dashboard, room catalogue or sequence
of labelled cards.

The chapter proves deeper recoverability through three things:

1. the basement architecture is built so one failing state can be isolated without
   taking the rest of the system with it;
2. Rollback treats the first failure as routine rather than exceptional; and
3. after joining, he immediately proceeds to another chamber to keep testing what
   survives when the next plan fails.

The implication is that Rollback has already considered failure-on-failure states
far beyond the one Patch can currently name.

## Explicitly removed beat

There is **no** CRT or synchronized-monitor `I'M IN` assent state.

The monitor may identify test state, containment state or useful operational
information, but it must not duplicate the meaning already carried by the dog-tag
handoff. The dog tags say the recurring assent phrase in Rollback's own material
language.

## Responsive survival requirement

The exact desktop camera arrangement is not sacred, but the **side-of-boundary
story** is.

At every supported width the chapter must preserve:

- an opening read from the failure/contained side of a reinforced boundary;
- Rollback as the calm, large safe-side mass with Patch secondary;
- a clear containment intervention;
- the shutter/occlusion beat and meaningful black interval;
- `What's your Plan B?` as the question carried across that interval;
- a return on the recoverable/safe side;
- Patch's `We don't have one` and Rollback's `Then you ain't got a plan` diagnosis;
- the wry realisation that Patch came for the missing capability;
- one unambiguous dog-tag assent; and
- Rollback already moving toward the next containment problem.

At 320 CSS px and actual 200% zoom, subordinate environmental detail may collapse,
but the boundary reversal and causal sequence must remain legible. Do not turn the
story into a stack of unrelated bunker cards.

At reduced motion, use static before/after warning states and discrete shutter
positions or equivalent authored frames. No story-critical meaning may depend on
the 5–10 second amber cycle.

## Approved composition invariants

- The basement is rebar-concrete containment space: constrained and almost
  stifling, but comfortably scaled for Rollback.
- Rollback is at the outer boundary of believable agent scale, not grotesque,
  human-scale or monstrous.
- The experiment itself remains off camera in the dominant opening. Reflection,
  light and environmental state imply the meltdown.
- The audience begins on the failure side of the glass and later returns on the
  recoverable side.
- Rollback's calm face through the observation window is more important than
  catastrophe spectacle.
- The amber warning cycle is the preferred continuous motion cue; the shutter is
  the decisive mechanical motion.
- `What's your Plan B?` lands over black after the shutter closes.
- `We don't have one.` and `Then you ain't got a plan.` are the approved exchange
  on the safe side.
- Patch's wry reaction produces Rollback's silent realisation that the missing
  recovery layer is why he is being recruited.
- Rollback never needs to say `you son of a gun. I'm in`; the two dog tags carry
  the phrase between them.
- The dog tags come off Rollback's own neck and land with physical weight.
- Rollback moves on to the next containment chamber rather than waiting for a
  recruitment celebration.

## Non-goals

- Do not show the failing experiment directly merely to create spectacle.
- Do not turn the chamber-side opening into horror, trapped-POV peril or panic.
- Do not restore CRT/synchronized-monitor `I'M IN`.
- Do not turn Rollback into a soldier, weapons character, military operator or
  intimidating brute.
- Do not make Plans B-Z a giant branching diagram, dashboard or labelled-room tour.
- Do not make Rollback create chaos in Silk's manner.
- Do not animate Rollback continuously or make every control/status element move.
- Do not make Rollback so large that he stops reading as a believable autonomous
  agent.
- Do not make the dog tags decorative prop-table jewellery; they come off his neck.
- Do not make the dog-tag handoff sentimental or ceremonial.
- Do not commission final imagery before the approved sequence has been proven in
  the live wireframe and its real media roles are known.

## Planning readiness

The chapter design is approved. A planner should not need to invent Rollback's
story role, audience-position device, basement pressure, scale relationship,
motion budget, shutter-to-black sequence, Plan B exchange, realisation beat,
dog-tag assent or exit behaviour.

The remaining uncertainty belongs to live wireframe/implementation work: exact
frame geometry, crops, shutter representation at each breakpoint, whether the
existing lockdown image earns a subordinate role, and which approved beats require
newly commissioned imagery.

Do **not** write Rollback's JIT implementation plan yet. The roadmap is sequential,
and Rollback's plan must consume the actual Klause milestone output and transition
seam rather than a hypothetical predecessor state.
