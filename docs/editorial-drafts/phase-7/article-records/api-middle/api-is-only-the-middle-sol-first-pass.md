## Manuscript

# The product is only the middle

*Seven and a half years ago, I thought engineering meant learning to write good software. I was not wrong. I had only mistaken an important part of the job for the whole thing.*

The code is the part we can see. It is where a ticket becomes something real, where an idea acquires constraints and where the craft is easiest to point at. Early in a career, that can make implementation look like the centre of the profession.

Experience has widened the picture for me. A product has a life before somebody opens an IDE, and it has a life after the code first works. The engineering lives in all of it.

## Before the middle

Before implementation, there is the quieter work of finding out what needs making. A request can arrive already phrased as a feature while still leaving the important questions unanswered. Who is trying to do what? What would make the outcome useful? Which existing behaviour or consumer constrains the answer? What is unsafe, expensive or simply not worth building?

None of that turns an engineer into a substitute product manager. It is part of treating the product as a real thing in the world rather than a shape to reproduce from a ticket. Good code cannot rescue a perfectly implemented misunderstanding.

The work often looks less dramatic from a distance. A conversation that clarifies a boundary, a small prototype that exposes an awkward constraint, or a decision to leave a tempting feature out can prevent more trouble than a clever implementation. The code still matters. It begins with a clearer job to do.

## The middle is still engineering

I do not mean that writing software is merely a delivery mechanism. Design, implementation and review are where engineering judgement becomes executable. An API contract, user journey, migration or operational control is a promise made concrete. It deserves care.

The mistake is to stop the boundary of responsibility at the pull request. A useful product has dependants, routes to production, assumptions and failure modes. Those things do not become somebody else's concern simply because the first version is green.

## After the middle

Shipping gives the work another life. It has to be deployed, understood, supported, observed and changed. A consumer may rely on a contract in a way the original implementation never made obvious. A migration may need a safe way back. A support question may reveal that a technically correct flow is unusable in practice.

This is where an engineer learns whether the thing they built can be owned. The answer is not always another feature. It may be a better diagnostic, a clearer handover, a smaller change or a decision to retire something that no longer earns its complexity.

I used to think those responsibilities appeared after the engineering was finished. They are how engineering finishes well.

## A wider boundary, not a higher perch

Growing into this view has not pulled me away from the work of building software. It has made the work more connected to the people and systems it affects. I still care about a clean implementation, a sensible contract and a test that can actually prove its claim. I now ask more often what came before the request, who will live with the result and what needs to be true when the next change arrives.

That is the direction I would offer someone earlier in the profession, gently rather than as a correction. Learn the code deeply. Take pride in it. Then keep looking beyond it. The product is only the middle of engineering.
