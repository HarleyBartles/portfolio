# The review graph

An iterative review only needs a graph when the next action has become a source of risk. A checklist is enough while one person can see the whole change and use ordinary judgement to decide what follows. It stops being enough when a controller can skip from a cheap scan to a broad review, fix a finding without checking the repair, or keep commissioning fresh opinions on its own edits. It then needs an explicit account of the review's state, its evidence, and its next permitted action.

The implemented workflow stores that account in a routing state schema. It holds the branch, base and head SHA, the current and previous nodes, the round, a fix-round cap, and the off-repo scratch location. `next_node.py` reads that state with the append-only records and allows one next node. That makes the order inspectable: deterministic preflight and scope-honesty precede `reviewer-fast`; the cheap pre-lens precedes matching deep reviewer lenses; triage decides whether a finding is worth repairing, deferring, or escalating. The graph earns its extra machinery by removing the controller's ability to treat those choices as interchangeable.

That isn't a claim that a graph is the right shape for every review. This workflow is deliberately long-running and inefficient when applied to frontier-model work. Its economics make more sense where relatively cheap agents can take narrow, recorded passes and the risk of unstructured rework is high enough to pay for the coordination. For a small change, a high-context reviewer may be cheaper and clearer. The useful question is whether explicit state and termination prevent a failure mode that the cheaper review passes would otherwise create.

## Repair is a different review from discovery

The graph separates finding discovery from repair verification. Reviewer lenses are selected from their declared file globs, keywords and inputs. `lens-triage` records a finding's lens, severity, discovery node and round in the finding ledger. It can resolve a false positive at triage, send an unresolved blocking or important finding through `metrics-track` to `finding-fix`, or stop at `blocked` when the issue is contested, tool-blocked or load-bearing.

The repair route is deliberately narrower than another whole-branch dispatch. A fix returns through `re-preflight`, then `reviewer-fixes` receives the originating lens's checklist, the original finding, the relevant slice of the branch diff and a fix diff limited to the repair's blast radius. It tests whether the intended repair holds without inviting every lens to generate new work from the whole branch. If the repair is non-trivial or cross-cutting, `regression-scan` gives `reviewer-strong` the touched area and classifies any confirmed new issue as same-lens, cross-lens or outside the blast radius.

Those routes need ledgers because a verbal status is too easy to blur. `record_finding.py`, `record_resolution.py` and `record_regression.py` build append-only records; `compile_metrics.py` turns them into review metrics. `resolved-ledger` is the single authority for recording a completed repair. It won't release the work to the final pass until every important or blocking finding has a resolution and no regression remains. A ledger cannot demonstrate that a fix is correct. It does make the claim about its route and current queue checkable.

## Termination is part of the engineering

The graph also says how review can stop without pretending the remaining uncertainty has disappeared. A finding that cannot be settled safely reaches `blocked`. A fix that exceeds its configured round limit reaches `blocked` too. These are useful outcomes. They preserve the question for a human instead of converting an unresolved disagreement into a tired, accidental approval.

The positive exit is equally narrow. After the repair queue is closed, `final-strong` runs one whole-branch `reviewer-strong` pass. A clean result, a clean preflight and, where repairs were made, the required ledger evidence permit `closeout`; closeout then checks that the planning artefacts are complete before `ready` runs the consumer CI gate, flips the draft PR to ready and waits for remote CI. The workflow's completion state therefore says more than "a reviewer found nothing on its last pass". It says that a particular sequence, with particular recorded inputs and routes, reached its stated gates.

It still falls short of a trustworthy-green claim. Coverage requires evidence that the relevant changed surfaces and review obligations were identified, not merely that a selector matched some reviewer profiles. Independence requires evidence that the reviewers capable of challenging a change were sufficiently separate in role, context and capability from the work they are asked to assess. The present selector can return no deep lenses when no profile matches, and the graph permits that route to continue. Its state machine demonstrates routing discipline rather than exhaustive coverage; a final-strong report is a bounded reviewer result rather than machine-proof of independent review.

A trustworthy completion claim has to name the exact source snapshot, branch and head SHA; the deterministic checks and reviewer logs actually run; every finding, resolution and regression; the coverage basis; and the independence basis. It must also disclose any unavailable reviewer, unreviewed surface, waiver or human escalation. Where those receipts are absent, the honest completion claim is smaller: the review followed its recorded graph as far as the available evidence shows. That outcome is worthwhile only when the workflow's cost has bought a real reduction in review risk rather than ceremony.
