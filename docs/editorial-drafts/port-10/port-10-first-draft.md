# I stopped choosing a font and started composing a word

I was comparing typefaces for **The Usual Specialists** when I noticed a name I knew.

Rian Hughes.

Chassis was already the strong favourite. As I remember it, Eurostile, Bank Gothic and Korolev had all been in the mix too. They were credible answers to the obvious brief: this is a solid crew doing a solid job, so the lettering should look solid.

Seeing Hughes's name did not make me choose Chassis.

It made me realise I had been asking the wrong question.

I had read Hughes talking years ago about typography as part of the total image. Not lettering as a label placed on top of the art, but letters as shapes with work to do. His name was enough to bring that way of looking back into focus.

So I stopped comparing fonts and asked instead: **how does the story earn the logo's sturdiness?**

That changed everything.

The Usual Specialists are six autonomous-agent specialists assembled for a lawful heist. Index finds the routes and establishes where they came from. Silk tries to break them. Writ establishes authority. Klause forces a decision. Rollback owns recovery. Receipt records what happened.

If they do all of that properly, the caper itself should be almost offensively boring.

That is why **THE USUAL** can afford to be small. It really is just the usual. Nothing dramatic to report.

**SPECIALISTS** carries nearly the whole mark because the Specialists carry nearly the whole job.

Once I looked at Chassis that way, the answer stopped being "this one looks rounder" or "this one feels heavier". I could see the crew inside the word.

I could see Silk anchoring a rappel line around one of those huge cut-outs and scaling the letter. I could see Rollback dragging a stroke forward as a containment barrier while the letters behind it blew themselves to pieces. I could see Index drawing routes through the negative space for Silk to test. I could see Klause putting one enormous K stamp across the surviving plan.

The typeface was not merely describing solidity any more. It had become somewhere the Specialists could work.

And then the name **Chassis** made a different kind of sense to me too.

To my eye, there is something in it of those enormous postwar American cars: rounded fenders, vast bonnets, heavy bodywork wrapped around machinery. I am not claiming that was Hughes's reference. It is simply what I see when I look at the forms. The letters have mass, but they also look assembled. There are cuts, apertures and joins. Something structural is happening inside all that weight.

That is exactly what I needed from the crew.

I had first encountered Rian Hughes thirty-four years earlier, at the tail end of being ten, in *Tales from Beyond Science* in *2000 AD*.

I loved those pages before I knew enough about design to explain why. I still do. I reread the stories every year or two and the artwork remains one of my favourite comic-book styles.

That childhood encounter was not the whole relationship. Later I read about Hughes's wider work and the thinking behind it. That impressed me even more than discovering that the artist I liked also designed fonts. What stuck was the comprehensiveness of it: illustration, lettering, logos and typography were not separate departments that happened to share a page. They could participate in one visual idea.

That was the useful memory when I saw his name under Chassis.

Not: *Rian Hughes designed this, therefore this must be the right font.*

More: *Hang on. I know how this guy thinks about letters. Stop shopping for a font. Look at the word as an image.*

There is a small accidental control case in the story. Korolev, another of the faces I remember considering, was also designed by Hughes. As I remember it, I had already ruled it out before noticing the connection. The designer's name was not functioning as a quality badge. One Hughes face could lose while another won.

Chassis won because it represented this story better.

That distinction matters to me because influence is easy to turn into imitation after the fact. I could tell a neat story in which ten-year-old me absorbed Rian Hughes, carried him silently for three decades and inevitably chose one of his typefaces when the moment arrived.

That is not how I remember it.

I liked Hughes's work as a child. Later I learned something from the way he thought. Much later still, I had my own design problem and one of his typefaces was already winning on its merits. Seeing his name reminded me of a useful way to inspect the work I was doing.

The surprise was not that an old influence had somehow made the decision for me.

It was that the old influence still had something useful to say when I was making the decision myself.

The wider Adventures of Patch identity makes that clearer. I did not discover Hughes and start putting Chassis everywhere. The PATCH mark uses a different face and solves a different problem. The supporting strapline became another composition again. Different parts of the identity had different jobs, so they got different answers.

That, more than using a particular Rian Hughes font, feels like the lesson I carried forward from him.

If the typography is in the picture, it is part of the picture.

Make it earn its place.
