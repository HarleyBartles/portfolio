# How The Invisibles’ logo designer influenced The Usual Specialists

Chassis was already winning.

I was looking for a face for **The Usual Specialists**. *[Internal link: meet the crew.]* The brief, at first, was pretty crude: solid caper, solid crew, solid word. **SPECIALISTS** needed to look like you could hang a tank off it and it wouldn’t budge. As I remember it, Eurostile, Bank Gothic and Korolev were all in the running. Chassis was ahead. *[Each face links out to a good public specimen rather than reproducing it here.]*

Then I noticed who designed it. Rian Hughes.

Hughes designed some very famous comic-book logos, *The Invisibles* among them. I couldn’t tell you what *The Invisibles* logo looks like. Maybe I’ve seen it and forgotten. Maybe I’ve never seen it. Dunno.

Rian Hughes, for me, starts in *2000 AD*, 1992, at the tail end of being ten. [*Tales from Beyond Science*](https://comicsalliance.com/tales-beyond-science-rian-hughes-mark-millar-preview/) looked angular as hell. Hard shapes, vivid blocks of colour, pages that didn’t look like anything else I was seeing in *2000 AD*. The stories had a bit of *The Twilight Zone* about them without feeling like somebody doing *The Twilight Zone* again, and some of them were pure nightmare fuel.

There was the one where a man works out what men’s nipples are for, cuts his eyes out, and can still see. Then there were the red men who came out of mirrors and took you away if you slipped up and fell asleep with a mirror facing into the room. Somewhere in there too: the mysterious missing sixth toe of Marilyn Monroe and the real whereabouts of the 27 Club.

That is an outrageous thing to put into a ten-year-old’s head. I loved it.

I picked up the whole set again, as I remember it, in my late twenties or early thirties. I’ve reread it every year or two since. Hughes never became one of those artists I remembered fondly and left in childhood. The pages still look brilliant to me.

Around the same period I was at [Brand Addition], working with logos and brand assets for clients including Google, Intel and a ton of others. I was around other people’s brand systems every day. That was when I started reading Hughes as a designer as well as the artist who’d drawn those nightmares.

He’s talked about treating type, illustration and graphic design as parts of the same composition. *[Link out to Hughes discussing this in his own words.]* What stuck was simpler: the letters are part of the picture. You can do something with them.

Now there’s his name underneath Chassis and I get a little *oh*. Right: stop shopping for fonts and look at the word.

I’d been asking whether the logo looked sturdy enough. Better question: **how does the story earn that sturdiness?**

The Specialists do all the work that makes the dangerous bit boring. Index maps the route. Silk tries to break it. Writ makes sure it’s lawful. Klause picks the plan. Rollback deals with whatever goes bang. Receipt makes sure nobody gets to rewrite history afterwards. Do that lot properly and the caper itself becomes almost offensively boring.

That’s the joke in the mark. **THE USUAL** stays small because it’s the boring intro. Just the usual. **SPECIALISTS** is massive because the Specialists are the job.

I looked at Chassis again and it got silly quickly. Silk could sling a rappel line around one of those cut-outs and disappear down the inside of a letter. Index could spread maps through the gaps and mark routes for Silk to try to break. If half the word blew up, Rollback could drag one of those great fat strokes across as a containment barrier. Klause could finish the whole thing with a big K stamp over whichever route survived. I could stage the caper inside the word. It appeared I’d found my answer.

Chassis also puts me in mind of those enormous old American cars. I keep wanting to say a sixties Oldsmobile and I’m probably wrong, but you know the sort of thing: rounded fenders, bonnet somewhere near your shoulder, more metal than seems strictly necessary. Then you get those enormous cuts through all the mass. Somewhere for the machinery to go. No idea whether Hughes had cars anywhere near his head when he drew it. That’s just where mine went.

The funny bit is that Korolev is Hughes too. As I remember it, I’d already ruled Korolev out before I noticed that. So much for destiny. Chassis was winning before the name meant anything. Seeing Hughes underneath it made me look properly at why.

**PATCH** went its own way. It ended up in ITC Serif Gothic Black, and the strapline became another composition again. Chassis stayed with The Usual Specialists.

Thirty-four years after those angular pages in *2000 AD*, I bought Chassis and built a word big enough for Silk to rappel through.

As I remember it, anyway.
