# Why ADRs?

## A love letter to a future engineer

Sometimes you're writing to your own future self.

You leave them enough of the argument to challenge your decision properly. The record serves whoever inherits the system, including when they decide the architecture should change.

I learned this by failing to write one.

## I didn't know the documentation was me

I hadn't designed the system alone. I was early in my career, left holding the baton, completely ill-equipped to explain the complex system I knew like the back of my hand.

The product that became LENS was the first substantial system I worked on as a professional software engineer. I joined Barbican Insurance in my first professional role as a Full Stack Software Engineer and, almost as soon as I arrived, talks began about Arch Capital acquiring the company. [The acquisition completed](https://ir.archgroup.com/news/news-details/2019/Arch-Capital-Group-Ltd--Closes-Acquisition-of-Barbican-Group-Holdings-Limited/default.aspx), and I moved to Arch with it. The product transferred too, as one of Barbican's assets. At the time, it was still called BBS, for Barbican Business Services.

What that formal acquisition meant for the part of Barbican I worked in was a change in ambition. The plan, as I understood it, was to take BBS beyond being an internal tool and sell it into the wider insurance market. It was an asset for a good reason.

[Public competition records describe Iris](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/54d2392fe5274a4514000001/Xchanging_initial_submission.pdf) as legacy policy-administration software for insurance and reinsurance underwriters. It handles serious work, but it expects users to understand a vast collection of opaque validation rules. The rules were expressed in reverse Polish notation. An engineer could inspect them with the validation-code manual open beside the screen. A business support operative couldn't reasonably be expected to do that while capturing a policy.

BBS put a modern application around that problem. Its name no longer worked after the acquisition, so Arch ran a naming contest. Mike, the senior engineer I worked with, and I submitted LENS and won a prize for it. LENS had no expanded form; the name described what the product did. It was a lens over Iris: it brought a blurry system into focus.

An operative selected a policy, or risk, from a queue and worked through roughly eight ordered sections of risk capture. A second operative received the completed risk in a separate peer-review queue. The review screen looked almost identical, but its inputs were disabled. The reviewer could mark corrections and return the risk for re-capture, or approve it for submission to Iris.

LENS mirrored the Iris validation rules as the operative worked. A field that would fail at submission showed its error beside the input. Nobody had to complete an entire risk, send it to Iris and then decipher why Iris refused it.

We made the user's job simpler by accepting more complexity into the system. Simplicity at the boundary was something the implementation had to earn.

## The architecture wasn't theatre

> Everybody thinks CQRS and event sourcing are theatre until somebody asks for a full audit history.

For plenty of systems, that criticism is fair. These patterns introduce concepts, infrastructure and failure modes that would be wasteful in a straightforward application. LENS had to reconstruct relevant actions and attribute them to an actor. The architecture was load-bearing.

I spent roughly two years on LENS in a three-person engineering team. Arch's framework had three software-engineer levels. I started at Level 1 and was promoted to Level 2 shortly before I left. Mike was Level 3, essentially its senior level.

I worked alongside Mike to implement LENS, drawing on his deep experience of domain-driven design, CQRS and event sourcing. At the time, those patterns were just the work in front of us. I was applying the practice before I understood how much engineering judgement sat behind it.

Risk capture, peer-review rejection, re-capture and approval were meaningful domain events. Reconstructing a policy required its history as well as its final state. We also used SQL Server Temporal Tables, which were very new at the time, to preserve historical row versions.

One LENS feature could show how the data on a page had changed between stages of the workflow. The operative could open a quick modal and see before-and-after field values. I can infer why we kept both forms of history. The event stream gave us authoritative, replayable domain state. Temporal tables made comparison between two points in time cheap enough for an interactive query.

It's a good explanation. I can't promise it was the team's explanation.

The code proves that both mechanisms existed. Whether my reconstruction matches the original decision was never recorded. That leaves a future engineer with a plausible explanation and no way to tell whether it is true.

## I knew the system. I couldn't transfer it

> You know DDD, right? It's textbook DDD.

I meant it as an opening: let's find the aggregates, follow the events and hand over the domain invariants together.

I only discovered how much of LENS I was carrying in my head when I decided to leave.

I had three months to hand over my knowledge. The handover consisted mostly of calls with a manager who was himself brand new. Diving through the code with me wasn't his job, and Arch didn't find an engineer to replace me until I was almost out of the door. The engineer-to-engineer handover barely happened.

I could name the architecture, point to it in the code and work fluently inside it. Producing a comprehensive account that could stand without me was a different skill, and I hadn't learned it yet. I was only about two years into my professional career. I didn't understand ADRs and I didn't know how to backfill documentation for a complex system.

What survived the handover was a list of nouns where the decisions should have been.

Knowing what I know now, I'd have treated those three months as a documentation recovery project. I wouldn't have tried to write an encyclopaedia of LENS. I'd have started with the decisions a competent engineer might reasonably question.

Why were risk capture and peer review separate gates when their screens looked almost identical? Why did the application reproduce Iris validation instead of relying on submission errors? Why did it keep an event stream and temporal row history? Which parts of the architecture protected real domain requirements, and which parts were merely the best choices we knew at the time?

Each question asked for the argument behind a pattern. The source code showed what we had built; my handover should have preserved why.

## The complexity hole was real

About a week before I left, I was told LENS would be sunset and replaced with something simpler. The expectation I was given was that the replacement would launch in six months.

I stayed in touch with a former colleague for roughly another year. When I lost contact with the story, the replacement still hadn't launched. The team had been iterating as it uncovered what seemed like a never-ending hole of domain complexity.

I don't know whether the replacement was eventually completed. I won't turn an incomplete account into a "we told you so" story. The part I can stand behind is narrower: LENS carried complexity because it modelled a genuinely complex domain and absorbed the difficulty of working with Iris. A new implementation remained free to choose different patterns while still having to carry those constraints.

An ADR could have separated those domain constraints from the implementation decisions that happened to carry them. It would have given the replacement team the reasoning to examine while leaving them free to replace LENS. That would have been a fairer inheritance than "it's textbook DDD."

## What an ADR owes its reader

A useful ADR gives a future engineer a fair starting point, with no obligation to obey us.

Recording only "we chose event sourcing" would have left the same hole as my handover: a decision with none of its reasoning.

For LENS, I'd have started with the boundary between risk capture and peer review. Both surfaces shared components: risk capture made them editable, while peer review presented the same data read-only and offered actions to return the risk for correction or approve it for submission to Iris.

The separation came from the business requirement for human review, enforced by the policy aggregate. A captured risk couldn't proceed to Iris until a second operative had approved it. A rejection returned it to risk capture.

An ADR would have told a future engineer where the freedom lay. The components, composition and even the wider interface could change while the invariant that every captured risk receives peer review before submission survived.

Mistakes belong in that record when they expose a boundary the polished final architecture hides. "We considered a simpler model" is useless if it omits the behaviour that made the model fail.

I reserve ADRs for routes a competent engineer could reasonably rediscover and propose again. The next engineer gets the evidence, including permission to conclude that the old team was wrong.

## Agents can infer a convincing wrong answer

Missing reasoning has become more dangerous now that agents can fill the silence.

An agent can inspect a codebase, recognise its patterns and produce a plausible explanation for why they exist. I just did the human version of that with LENS's event stream and temporal tables. The explanation may be correct, but the decision history is still missing.

I haven't solved ADR discipline for agents. Not all of my repositories have an ADR log, and agents don't yet identify every architectural decision reliably enough for me to trust the process without supervision. I still have to nudge them: this looks architectural, check whether an ADR should be created or updated.

I want an agent approaching an architectural boundary to find the existing rationale before it changes the code, then update that rationale when the decision changes. The agent should treat the ADR as evidence it can challenge.

I couldn't leave that kind of letter for the engineer who inherited LENS. Now the next engineer may be another person, my future self or an agent working under supervision. Whoever it is, they should inherit more than code and a list of nouns. They should inherit the argument.
